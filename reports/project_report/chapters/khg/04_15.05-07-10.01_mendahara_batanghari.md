<!-- Writing prompt: Where the KHG lies, how much of it is peat, how it ranks nationally and why, what canal network and blocks it has, and which elevation models and steps have run. -->

Mendahara-Batanghari is a *KHG* (*Kesatuan Hidrologis Gambut*, peat hydrological unit) in Jambi province. Of its 2,013 km², 1,731 km² is peat, about 86 % of the polygon. With the default weights proposed for agreement with KLH and the World Bank it ranks 71 of 476 ranked KHG, in the high-priority class. The land is heavily drained: the mapped network has 860 km of primary, 1,338 km of secondary and 2,388 km of tertiary canals, about 2.3 km of canal per km² of KHG (4,586 km over 2,013 km²). The national layer records 700 water-management structures, 417 of them realised canal blocks. Airborne LiDAR covers the whole KHG, so this is one of the sites where DeltaDTM and DEMNAS can be judged against LiDAR everywhere, and every step from terrain to canal blocks has run on all three elevation models. Of the 14 SiTMAT water-table stations in the KHG, 12 have records for the dry-season deficit.

### Terrain and the three elevation models

{{figure: khg_dem_maps:15.05-07-10.01 | Mendahara-Batanghari: ground elevation from each elevation model on one colour scale, and their differences. Source: LiDAR terrain model, DeltaDTM and DEMNAS after the DEM correction | made by khg_dem_maps in re-peat/tools/project_report_khg.py from the KHG work folder}}

<!-- Writing prompt: Describe the terrain (dome, river levees, coast) and where the models disagree, for example along rivers, plantations or forest edges, and the likely reason (canopy left in the surface, coarse resolution, datum). -->

On LiDAR the highest ground forms a band across the south-west and centre of the KHG, with a second raised area in the eastern lobe and a lower dome in the northern lobe. The outer margins of the northern and eastern lobes are the lowest ground. DeltaDTM draws the same shape higher almost everywhere (median difference 0.97 m, more than 2 m above LiDAR over 12 % of the KHG), and departs from that offset mainly along the south-western and western edge, where it lies well above LiDAR. DEMNAS disagrees in large patches with straight edges: much of the interior of the northern lobe and parts of the south-west sit above LiDAR, while blocks in the centre sit below it. DEMNAS is more than 2 m above LiDAR over 29 % of the KHG and more than 2 m below it over 0.7 %. Patches that follow land-cover boundaries in this way point to canopy left in the surface. Tree cover is 82 % of the benchmark area here, and the correction fitted a canopy share of only 0.087 for tree cover in DEMNAS (chapter 4).

Source: LiDAR terrain model, DeltaDTM and DEMNAS after the DEM correction | made by khg_dem_stats in re-peat/tools/project_report_khg.py from the KHG work folder}}

<!-- Writing prompt: Compare relief and the median and spread of the difference to LiDAR; say whether the difference is a constant offset (datum) or local error, and what that means for the slopes the later steps use. -->

Relief (5th to 95th percentile) is 0.9 to 7.8 m on LiDAR, 1.9 to 9.0 m on DeltaDTM and 1.4 to 9.8 m on DEMNAS, so DEMNAS stretches the range by about 1.5 m. Both corrected models sit above LiDAR by a similar median, 0.97 m for DeltaDTM and 0.86 m for DEMNAS. The spread around that median differs far more: the NMAD is 0.64 m for DeltaDTM and 1.60 m for DEMNAS. The DeltaDTM difference is mostly a constant offset, as at the other four KHG with LiDAR, and a constant offset leaves slopes unchanged. The DEMNAS difference is local error four times the 0.40 m head step used to place canal blocks, and that error enters every slope the later steps compute.

### Sub-KHG units

{{figure: khg_units_maps:15.05-07-10.01 | Mendahara-Batanghari: sub-KHG units delineated on each elevation model. Source: sub-KHG delineation on each elevation model, mapped canals | made by khg_units_maps in re-peat/tools/project_report_khg.py from the KHG work folder}}

<!-- Writing prompt: How many units each model gives, how their boundaries follow canals and rivers, and where the models draw them differently and why. -->

Each model gives 29 *sub-KHG* units, close to the cap of 30. The KHG has 2,190 primary and secondary canal segments, so the catchment pieces are small and the cap is reached at a minimum unit area of about 30 km² (chapter 5). Many boundaries run straight along primary and secondary canals, especially in the canal grids of the north and east, and these are drawn alike on all three models. The models diverge where boundaries cross open land between canal blocks, in the centre and the west, because there the divide follows the terrain itself. DeltaDTM merges more of that ground into one large unit (203 km² at most, against 140 km² on LiDAR), and DEMNAS cuts the eastern lobe into smaller units than LiDAR.

Agreement is moderate. LiDAR and DeltaDTM agree best, with 76 % of the area in the best-matching unit and an adjusted Rand index of 0.59. LiDAR and DEMNAS reach 71 % and 0.55, and DeltaDTM and DEMNAS differ most, at 70 % and 0.54. Median unit area is 64 km² on LiDAR, 55 km² on DeltaDTM and 56 km² on DEMNAS.

### Water balance and deficit

{{table: khg_water:15.05-07-10.01 | Mendahara-Batanghari: dry-season deficit from water-table stations and climatic balance per sub-KHG. Source: SiTMAT water-table stations, CHIRPS rain, MOD16 and WaPOR evapotranspiration | made by khg_water in re-peat/tools/project_report_khg.py from the KHG work folder}}

<!-- Writing prompt: State the deficit and its period, the months in deficit, and whether the order of units by balance is usable for each model; explain any model whose order test fails. -->

From 1 June to 13 October 2026 the *neraca air* (water balance) at the 12 stations gives a dry-season deficit of 119 mm (108 to 130 mm for specific yield 0.10 to 0.20), or 240 million m³ over the KHG, with 4 months in deficit. The climate removed 152 mm in that window; the stations fell 22 cm, which supplied 33 mm, and the rest had to come from outside the rain balance (chapter 8). Seven of the 12 stations belong to one concession, so the figure leans towards managed land. The order of units by climatic balance is not usable on any model: MOD16 and WaPOR rank the units in unrelated ways (Spearman -0.10 to 0.08), and the MOD16 balance follows forest share almost entirely, so interception sets the order rather than water (chapter 8).

Source: CHIRPS rain and MOD16 evapotranspiration, 2018-2025 | made by khg_water_fig in re-peat/tools/project_report_khg.py from the KHG work folder}}

<!-- Writing prompt: Name the dry months, and say whether the elevation model changes the balance (it enters only through the units). -->

In the mean year 2018-2025 July is the only month with a negative climatic balance, about -38 mm. February and August stay low but positive (16 and 13 mm). The KHG mean is 62.0 mm/month with MOD16 on every model, because the elevation model enters only through the unit boundaries. Those boundaries widen or narrow the spread between units: 53.4 to 74.8 mm/month on LiDAR, 51.5 to 78.9 mm/month on DeltaDTM and 48.3 to 73.6 mm/month on DEMNAS. Deficit months per year average 2.46 to 2.48 on all three.

### Priority of sub-KHG

{{figure: khg_priority_maps:15.05-07-10.01 | Mendahara-Batanghari: need for intervention per sub-KHG, by elevation model. Source: sub-KHG prioritisation with default weights | made by khg_priority_maps in re-peat/tools/project_report_khg.py from the KHG work folder}}

<!-- Writing prompt: Where the high-need units are and what drives them (fire, canals, dryness), and whether the same places come out on top with every model. Mention that the weights are the defaults proposed for agreement with KLH and the World Bank. -->

With the default weights proposed for agreement with KLH and the World Bank, the high-need units lie in the eastern lobe and the centre, and most of the northern lobe and the south-west are low need on every model. All three models put the top-ranked units in the eastern lobe. These units have the highest hotspot density and burned share of the KHG; high-need units further down the list get there mostly through dense canals and wide canal reach. Each model selects a different set of three: LiDAR spreads its top three from the centre to the far east, while DeltaDTM and DEMNAS each pair two units in the far east with one unit further west.

<!-- Writing prompt: Say how much area keeps its class across models and how strongly the scores agree; explain the largest difference. -->

Every model rates 10 units as high need, covering 695 km² on LiDAR, 754 km² on DeltaDTM and 616 km² on DEMNAS. LiDAR and DEMNAS agree best: 84 % of the area keeps its class, 80 % of the LiDAR high-need area is also high need on DEMNAS, and the area-weighted Spearman correlation of scores is 0.91. DeltaDTM is the outlier, with 67 % of the area in the same class as LiDAR and 69 % as DEMNAS. Its largest difference is a large high-need unit in the west-central part that LiDAR and DEMNAS split into smaller medium-need units, so a single boundary choice moves the class of a large area.

### Canal blocks and pumping

{{figure: khg_blocks_maps:15.05-07-10.01 | Mendahara-Batanghari: proposed canal blocks in the three highest-ranked sub-KHG, by elevation model. Source: RE-PEAT Interventions on each elevation model, mapped canals and existing blocks | made by khg_blocks_maps in re-peat/tools/project_report_khg.py from the KHG work folder}}

<!-- Writing prompt: Where the blocks go and why there (canal slope, burned land), how many are dropped as low benefit, and how the selected units and block positions change with the model. -->

RE-PEAT Interventions places *sekat kanal* (canal blocks) by the 0.40 m head rule along the analysed canals of the three selected units, then keeps the fewest that give 90 % of the canal rewetting. On LiDAR it proposes 112 blocks and keeps 64, most of them on the canal grid of the far east. DeltaDTM proposes 114 and keeps 54, and DEMNAS proposes 216 and keeps 73, so 48, 60 and 143 blocks are dropped as low benefit. Because DeltaDTM and DEMNAS select a unit in the west, their blocks fall in two separate clusters, and the western cluster sits next to existing blocks (35, 30 and 48 existing blocks lie on the analysed canals). DEMNAS proposes almost twice as many blocks as LiDAR, partly because its local error steepens canal slopes and partly because its selected units differ.

{{table: khg_interventions:15.05-07-10.01 | Mendahara-Batanghari: canal blocks, water held and budget, by elevation model. Source: RE-PEAT Interventions and sub-KHG prioritisation on each elevation model | made by khg_interventions in re-peat/tools/project_report_khg.py from the KHG work folder}}

<!-- Writing prompt: Give the blocks proposed and kept, water held against the deficit, and the central budget with its main cost item; say what drives the cost differences between models. -->

The kept blocks hold 4.47 million m³ on LiDAR, 3.45 million m³ on DeltaDTM and 4.98 million m³ on DEMNAS, against a deficit of the selected units of 38.2, 26.2 and 28.8 million m³. That is about 12 %, 13 % and 17 % of the deficit. The central budget is 14 billion Rp on LiDAR, 77 billion Rp on DeltaDTM and 130 billion Rp on DEMNAS. On DeltaDTM and DEMNAS, revegetation of burned land is the largest item (57 and 70 billion Rp), because their selected units include burned land assigned that treatment; on LiDAR revegetation costs nothing, and most of the budget is the KLH estimate of blocks missing outside the analysed canals of the selected units, an item that also grows with the noisier slopes of DEMNAS. Pumping was not assessed in this analysis, so no pump design exists for this KHG.

### How the elevation model changes the result

{{table: khg_dem_effect:15.05-07-10.01 | Mendahara-Batanghari: headline outputs by elevation model and their spread. Source: RE-PEAT results on each elevation model | made by khg_dem_effect in re-peat/tools/project_report_khg.py from the KHG work folder}}

<!-- Writing prompt: Which outputs move most with the elevation model and which barely move; for this KHG, which model you would use for decisions and why. -->

The number of units (29), the KHG mean climatic balance (range 0.1 mm/month), the station deficit and the number of high-need units (10) do not move with the elevation model. The high-need area moves by a factor of 1.2 and the water held by 1.4. The outputs that depend on slope move most. Blocks needed by the slope rule of the ranking range from 1,995 on LiDAR to 8,763 on DEMNAS (a factor of 4.4), blocks proposed by a factor of 1.9, and the central budget by 9.3, from 14 to 130 billion Rp. The budget spread comes from two effects together: the noisier DEMNAS slopes, and the different selected units, which bring burned land into the DeltaDTM and DEMNAS plans.

LiDAR covers the whole KHG and is the model to use for decisions here. DeltaDTM is the closest substitute: its 0.97 m offset leaves slopes intact, its NMAD of 0.64 m is the lowest of the corrected models, and its priority ranking still correlates with LiDAR at 0.87, although its units and selected units differ. DEMNAS ranks the units much like LiDAR (Spearman 0.91), but its 1.60 m local error is four times the 0.40 m head step between blocks and should not be used to place blocks or count them.

### Summary for this KHG

<!-- Writing prompt: Three to five sentences: the main finding for rewetting in this KHG, the recommended elevation model, the proposed blocks and budget, and what is still missing (data or steps not yet run). -->

Mendahara-Batanghari is a densely drained KHG that needed 119 mm of water from outside the rain balance in the 2026 dry season, 240 million m³ over 2,013 km², and its highest-need units lie in the burned eastern lobe on every elevation model. On the recommended LiDAR model the plan keeps 64 canal blocks in the three selected units, for a central budget of 14 billion Rp, and these blocks hold about 12 % of those units' deficit, so blocking alone cannot close it. The ranking is fairly stable across models, but block counts and the budget are not: DEMNAS raises the budget to 130 billion Rp through noisier slopes and a different choice of units. Pumping was not assessed in this analysis. The station deficit rests on 12 stations, 7 of them in one concession, so the need of the land outside licences is the least certain part of the estimate.

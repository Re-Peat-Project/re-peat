<!-- Writing prompt: Where the KHG lies, how much of it is peat, how it ranks nationally and why, what canal network and blocks it has, and which elevation models and steps have run. -->

Saleh-Sugihan is a *KHG* (*Kesatuan Hidrologis Gambut*, peat hydrological unit) of 1,898 km² in Sumatera Selatan, of which 1,607 km² is peat. It ranks 30th of 476 KHG nationally, in the high-priority class, with default weights proposed for agreement with KLH and the World Bank. The rank comes from fire history: the 2015 fires burned 42.6 % of its peat, and it burned again in 2019 (28.3 %) and 2023 (11.9 %), which puts it at the 99th percentile for burned share and repeat burns (chapter 6). On fire history alone it would rise to 23rd; on this year's hotspots alone it falls to 151st.

The KHG is heavily drained, with 321 km of primary, 1,156 km of secondary and 2,171 km of tertiary canals. The national layer records 192 water-management structures, 25 of them realised canal blocks. No SiTMAT water-table station lies inside the KHG. LiDAR covers about 62 % of the area, the southern part, so the LiDAR results describe only that part. The full chain has run on all three elevation models: LiDAR, DeltaDTM and DEMNAS.

### Terrain and the three elevation models

{{figure: khg_dem_maps:16.02-07.01 | Saleh-Sugihan: ground elevation from each elevation model on one colour scale, and their differences. Source: LiDAR terrain model, DeltaDTM and DEMNAS after the DEM correction | made by khg_dem_maps in re-peat/tools/project_report_khg.py from the KHG work folder}}

<!-- Writing prompt: Describe the terrain (dome, river levees, coast) and where the models disagree, for example along rivers, plantations or forest edges, and the likely reason (canopy left in the surface, coarse resolution, datum). -->

The land rises from the low coastal plain in the north to a peat dome in the south, where all three models show the highest ground. DeltaDTM sits above LiDAR almost everywhere, by a fairly even amount. DEMNAS behaves differently in the two halves of the LiDAR area. In the northern part, where the canal grid is dense, DEMNAS lies below LiDAR and shows diagonal stripes that do not follow the land, which points to an artefact of the national surface model. Over the southern dome it lies above LiDAR in patches, and over the LiDAR area as a whole it is more than 2 m above LiDAR in 27 % of the cells and more than 2 m below in 1 %, the pattern expected where tree canopy is left in a surface model.

{{table: khg_dem_stats:16.02-07.01 | Saleh-Sugihan: resolution, cover and relief of each elevation model, and its difference to LiDAR. Source: LiDAR terrain model, DeltaDTM and DEMNAS after the DEM correction | made by khg_dem_stats in re-peat/tools/project_report_khg.py from the KHG work folder}}

<!-- Writing prompt: Compare relief and the median and spread of the difference to LiDAR; say whether the difference is a constant offset (datum) or local error, and what that means for the slopes the later steps use. -->

Within the 5th to 95th percentiles, LiDAR spans 0.6 to 6.7 m over its 62 % of the KHG, DeltaDTM 2.2 to 7.7 m and DEMNAS -0.3 to 8.6 m over the whole KHG. DeltaDTM lies a median 1.26 m above LiDAR with a spread (NMAD) of 0.56 m. This is mostly an offset, as at the other four KHG with LiDAR, and an offset does not change slopes. DEMNAS has a smaller median difference, 0.96 m, but a spread of 1.77 m, about three times that of DeltaDTM, and its differences fall into two groups, one below and one above LiDAR. This local error goes straight into the canal slopes that set block spacing, so DEMNAS slopes at this KHG are the least reliable of the three.

### Sub-KHG units

{{figure: khg_units_maps:16.02-07.01 | Saleh-Sugihan: sub-KHG units delineated on each elevation model. Source: sub-KHG delineation on each elevation model, mapped canals | made by khg_units_maps in re-peat/tools/project_report_khg.py from the KHG work folder}}

<!-- Writing prompt: How many units each model gives, how their boundaries follow canals and rivers, and where the models draw them differently and why. -->

LiDAR gives 29 *sub-KHG* units, DeltaDTM 26 and DEMNAS 27, all close to the cap of 30. The KHG has 3,080 primary and secondary canal segments (chapter 5), so unit boundaries mostly follow canals: in the north they trace the plantation grid, and in the middle all three models draw the same east-west boundaries along long straight canals. The models part ways on the southern dome, where canals are sparse and the drainage divides come from the terrain itself. The LiDAR units cover only 1,174 km², so they are smaller (median 39 km² against 75 km² on DeltaDTM and 67 km² on DEMNAS) and leave the northern part of the KHG without units.

DeltaDTM and DEMNAS agree most closely: 79 % of the area lies in the best-matching unit, with an adjusted Rand index of 0.68. Each agrees less with LiDAR over the LiDAR area, with 65 % and 0.47 for DeltaDTM and 68 % and 0.49 for DEMNAS. LiDAR against DeltaDTM is the pair that differs most. Part of that difference comes from the LiDAR delineation stopping at the edge of the LiDAR, which cuts units that the other two models extend northwards.

### Water balance and deficit

{{table: khg_water:16.02-07.01 | Saleh-Sugihan: dry-season deficit from water-table stations and climatic balance per sub-KHG. Source: SiTMAT water-table stations, CHIRPS rain, MOD16 and WaPOR evapotranspiration | made by khg_water in re-peat/tools/project_report_khg.py from the KHG work folder}}

<!-- Writing prompt: State the deficit and its period, the months in deficit, and whether the order of units by balance is usable for each model; explain any model whose order test fails. -->

No water-table station lies inside Saleh-Sugihan, so no dry-season deficit from stations can be computed for this KHG, and no water need can be set against the blocks. The climatic balance from the *neraca air* (water balance), CHIRPS rain minus MOD16 evapotranspiration for 2018-2025, averages 84.2 mm/month over the whole KHG with DeltaDTM or DEMNAS units, with about 2.2 months in deficit a year. The LiDAR units give 75.5 mm/month and 2.46 deficit months because they cover only the drier southern part. The order of units by balance is usable only with the LiDAR units, where MOD16 and WaPOR rank the units alike (Spearman 0.56 against 0.43 needed, chapter 8). With the DeltaDTM and DEMNAS units their agreement, 0.38 and 0.30, stays below the 0.46 and 0.44 needed, so for the whole KHG the order of units by balance should not be used.

{{figure: khg_water_fig:16.02-07.01 | Saleh-Sugihan: climatic balance through the mean year, and the balance of each sub-KHG, by elevation model. Source: CHIRPS rain and MOD16 evapotranspiration, 2018-2025 | made by khg_water_fig in re-peat/tools/project_report_khg.py from the KHG work folder}}

<!-- Writing prompt: Name the dry months, and say whether the elevation model changes the balance (it enters only through the units). -->

The dry months are July, August and September, when the mean balance falls to about zero over the whole KHG and below zero over the LiDAR part. As in the rest of South Sumatra, the dry season can run from July to October; 2019 was the worst year and 2022 had no deficit month here (chapter 8). The elevation model enters the balance only through the units: DeltaDTM and DEMNAS give the same KHG mean, and their unit means span almost the same range, 68.0 to 113.1 and 68.0 to 113.0 mm/month.

### Priority of sub-KHG

{{figure: khg_priority_maps:16.02-07.01 | Saleh-Sugihan: need for intervention per sub-KHG, by elevation model. Source: sub-KHG prioritisation with default weights | made by khg_priority_maps in re-peat/tools/project_report_khg.py from the KHG work folder}}

<!-- Writing prompt: Where the high-need units are and what drives them (fire, canals, dryness), and whether the same places come out on top with every model. Mention that the weights are the defaults proposed for agreement with KLH and the World Bank. -->

With the default weights proposed for agreement with KLH and the World Bank, every model puts the high-need units on the southern dome and along the band of long east-west canals in the middle of the KHG. Burned land makes up most of the treatment cost in the selected units (see below), which fits the KHG's fire history. DeltaDTM and DEMNAS also mark a high-need unit in the north-west, which lies outside the LiDAR. All three models place one of their top three units on the south-western edge. LiDAR and DEMNAS also select a unit in the middle band, while DeltaDTM takes its other two from the dome further south and east.

The need scores agree well across models, with an area-weighted rank correlation of 0.85 to 0.86 for every pair. Between 71 % and 79 % of the shared area keeps its need class, and 79 % to 88 % of the area that is high need on one model is high need on the other. LiDAR and DEMNAS differ most in class (71 % the same). The high-need area is 460 km² on LiDAR, which covers only part of the KHG, against 638 km² on DeltaDTM and 618 km² on DEMNAS.

### Canal blocks and pumping

{{figure: khg_blocks_maps:16.02-07.01 | Saleh-Sugihan: proposed canal blocks in the three highest-ranked sub-KHG, by elevation model. Source: RE-PEAT Interventions on each elevation model, mapped canals and existing blocks | made by khg_blocks_maps in re-peat/tools/project_report_khg.py from the KHG work folder}}

<!-- Writing prompt: Where the blocks go and why there (canal slope, burned land), how many are dropped as low benefit, and how the selected units and block positions change with the model. -->

On every model most proposed *sekat kanal* (canal blocks) sit on the canal along the south-western edge of the KHG, where the land rises enough along the canal for the head rule to place blocks. LiDAR keeps 22 of 59 proposed blocks, DeltaDTM 11 of 21 and DEMNAS 30 of 68; the rest add little to the canal rewetting and are dropped. The other selected units change with the model. On LiDAR the second cluster lies on a canal in the east of the middle band; on DEMNAS blocks follow two east-west canals in the middle band; on DeltaDTM the two other selected units, larger and further east, receive no blocks. None of the 25 existing blocks lies on the analysed canals. Pumping was not assessed in this analysis.

{{table: khg_interventions:16.02-07.01 | Saleh-Sugihan: canal blocks, water held and budget, by elevation model. Source: RE-PEAT Interventions and sub-KHG prioritisation on each elevation model | made by khg_interventions in re-peat/tools/project_report_khg.py from the KHG work folder}}

<!-- Writing prompt: Give the blocks proposed and kept, water held against the deficit, and the central budget with its main cost item; say what drives the cost differences between models. -->

The kept blocks hold 1.24 million m³ on LiDAR, 0.48 million m³ on DeltaDTM and 1.56 million m³ on DEMNAS. Without a station deficit, this water cannot be compared with the need of the selected units. The central budget is 23 billion Rp on LiDAR, 25 billion Rp on DeltaDTM and 31 billion Rp on DEMNAS. Revegetation of burned land is the main item: 25 and 31 billion Rp, nearly all the budget, on DeltaDTM and DEMNAS, and 14 billion Rp on LiDAR, where most of the remaining 9 billion Rp (the difference of the two) is the KLH estimate of missing blocks in a unit given block treatment (chapter 9). The budget therefore follows how much burned land the selected units contain rather than the number of blocks.

The ranking's slope rule, applied to all primary and secondary canals of the KHG, gives a very different picture: 7,752 blocks needed on LiDAR (62 % of the KHG), 6,768 on DEMNAS and only 1,165 on DeltaDTM. The smooth 30 m DeltaDTM gives gentle canal slopes and wide spacing, while the local error in DEMNAS and the fine detail of the 20 m LiDAR give steeper slopes and close spacing. Chapter 7 treats this count as a first, rough figure.

### How the elevation model changes the result

{{table: khg_dem_effect:16.02-07.01 | Saleh-Sugihan: headline outputs by elevation model and their spread. Source: RE-PEAT results on each elevation model | made by khg_dem_effect in re-peat/tools/project_report_khg.py from the KHG work folder}}

<!-- Writing prompt: Which outputs move most with the elevation model and which barely move; for this KHG, which model you would use for decisions and why. -->

Between DeltaDTM and DEMNAS, which both cover the whole KHG, the block counts move most: 5.8 times for the slope-rule count, 3.2 times for proposed blocks, 2.7 times for kept blocks and 3.3 times for the water they hold. The unit count, the climatic balance, the high-need area and the central budget (1.3 times) barely move. Some of the block spread comes from the choice of units, since each model selects different top-three units, and some from terrain: DEMNAS carries a local error of 1.77 m (NMAD) against LiDAR, larger than the 0.40 m step of the head rule.

For decisions, LiDAR is the model to use in the southern 62 % of the KHG, which holds the dome and most of the high-need area. For the northern part, DeltaDTM is the better of the two open models here: its difference to LiDAR is mostly an offset, while DEMNAS shows stripes and a wide spread. Its low block counts should still be read as the lower end of a range, with DEMNAS as the upper end, until the north is surveyed.

### Summary for this KHG

<!-- Writing prompt: Three to five sentences: the main finding for rewetting in this KHG, the recommended elevation model, the proposed blocks and budget, and what is still missing (data or steps not yet run). -->

Saleh-Sugihan needs rewetting chiefly on its southern dome, where repeated fires since 2015 have left the most burned peat, and every elevation model ranks that area high. On LiDAR, which covers that part, the plan keeps 22 canal blocks in the three selected units, holding 1.24 million m³, for a central budget of 23 billion Rp, most of it revegetation of burned land. LiDAR is the recommended model for the south and DeltaDTM for the north. The units and priorities hold up across models, but block counts vary up to 5.8 times. With no water-table station in the KHG, the water held by blocks cannot be compared with a dry-season deficit, the northern 38 % has no LiDAR, and pumping was not assessed in this analysis.
